INFORMATION WORLD MODEL
Master Product & Architecture Definition — v1.0
Status: Foundational product decisions established. Technical implementation proposed, pending experiments.

Objective: Build a proprietary intelligence system that represents, connects, interprets, and learns from information about the world, allowing it to reconstruct historical developments, understand relationships, identify emerging changes, and generate increasingly useful predictions.

PART I — WHAT WE ARE BUILDING
1. The core vision
The world generates information continuously.

People exchange ideas, develop beliefs, establish relationships, make decisions, invest money, create companies, publish research, and influence one another.

These activities are connected.

An executive's decision may depend on a conversation, market conditions, financial incentives, previous experiences, or information encountered in an entirely different industry.

The resulting decision can influence companies, capital allocation, technologies, communities, and public narratives.

Today, these connections are fragmented across different sources and information systems.

We want to build an evolving model of these relationships and the mechanisms through which they produce changes in the world.

Our system must be capable of answering five fundamental questions:

What happened?

What is connected to what?

Why might these relationships and events have occurred?

What can historical evidence teach us?

What might happen next, and how can we test that prediction?

The objective isn't to know everything.

It is to extract the greatest possible reliable understanding from the information available.

2. What the system is NOT
It is not simply:

A social listening platform.

A financial database.

A personal memory chatbot.

A competitor monitoring dashboard.

A graph database connected to an LLM.

A collection of autonomous agents.

A tool that claims to know what everyone privately thinks.

These can be features, reference systems, or specialized applications.

Our actual product is the underlying information and inference architecture that allows these capabilities to work together.

3. Fundamental principles
Principle 1 — Relationships are foundational.

People, information, transactions, organizations, conversations, technology, and economic outcomes should not exist in isolated systems.

Principle 2 — No information domain has universal priority.

Money trails aren't more important than people. People aren't more important than economic outcomes. Their relative significance depends on the investigation.

Principle 3 — The model must understand nuances.

A company discussing an acquisition, considering an acquisition, announcing an acquisition, and completing an acquisition are different things.

Principle 4 — Evidence and inference are different.

The system can investigate uncertain relationships without declaring them factual.

Principle 5 — Historical learning is essential.

Every prediction should be testable against subsequent outcomes, and every failed explanation should help improve the system.

Principle 6 — Intelligence must be economical.

The model should retrieve and process the information needed for an investigation, not its entire database.

Principle 7 — Architecture should be expandable.

New information types, sources, domains, models, analytical methods, and user interfaces must fit without rebuilding the foundation.

Principle 8 — Human expertise matters.

We need to study how experienced researchers, investors, economists, consultants, executives, and other specialists evaluate information and make decisions.

PART II — THE INFORMATION MODEL
4. What exists in the world model?
Our initial analytical dimensions are:

Dimension	Examples
People	Professional roles, public activity, relationships, decisions
Information	Ideas, claims, publications, narratives, evidence
Economics	Capital, transactions, financial incentives, demand
Organizations	Companies, institutions, teams, ownership structures
Environments	Industries, technologies, communities, markets
Time	Historical events, sequences, changes, outcomes
These are not permanent categories.

They describe dimensions of information, not isolated databases.

We should be able to add geography, regulation, infrastructure, products, scientific research, and other domains.

5. The foundational ontology
I propose twelve foundational object types.

Object	What it represents
Entity	Something identifiable: person, company, product, technology, community
Observation	Information captured from a specific source
Assertion	A statement about the world made by a source or extractor
Event	Something that occurred, was announced, or was reported
Relationship	A typed, contextual connection between objects
Context	Conditions that affect interpretation: domain, time, situation, access
Hypothesis	A proposed explanation of evidence
Framework	A reusable analytical or decision-making methodology
Decision	A documented choice made by a person or organization
Outcome	A subsequent observable result
Evidence record	Source provenance and support for an assertion or hypothesis
Analysis record	How the system investigated a question and reached its result
These objects work together.

For example:

A person participates in a conversation. The conversation contains claims. The claims relate to a technology. An organization announces an investment. A hypothesis connects the earlier conversation with the investment.

The system preserves each element independently.

It does not assume the conversation caused the investment.

6. How relationships work
There will be different relationship families.

Structural relationships

Employment, ownership, membership, partnership, organizational roles.

Information relationships

Published, cited, referenced, repeated, challenged, interpreted, responded to.

Economic relationships

Invested, acquired, supplied, contracted, financed, purchased, committed capital.

Social and professional relationships

Collaborated, attended the same event, participated in a public discussion, formally advised.

Temporal relationships

Preceded, followed, overlapped, changed, replaced, became inactive.

Explanatory relationships

Potentially influenced, possibly contributed to, likely depended on, may share a common cause.

The final category is different.

An explanatory relationship is a hypothesis until its evidence supports a stronger conclusion.

Complex interactions should allow multiple participants and roles, rather than forcing everything into two-node connections.

7. Identity resolution
Names are not identities.

Every entity gets a stable internal identifier.

For example:

person_000104
organization_000078
event_000912

A person can have multiple public handles, aliases, or historical professional affiliations.

Those identifiers are mapped to the same entity only when the evidence is sufficient.

Uncertain matches must remain separate until resolved.

An incorrect merge must be reversible without destroying the original evidence.

PART III — THE MEMORY SYSTEM
8. Memory architecture
We will not build one enormous database that every model query must traverse.

Instead, we will maintain a shared evidence and identity foundation with specialized, connected information partitions.

The structure:

SHARED FOUNDATION
    |
    |-- Entity Registry
    |-- Source / Evidence Ledger
    |-- Temporal Event Ledger
    |-- Assertion Registry
    |-- Permission Registry
    |
    +-- INFORMATION PARTITIONS
            |
            |-- People & Professional Networks
            |-- Narratives & Publications
            |-- Financial & Economic Networks
            |-- Organizations & Markets
            |-- Technology & Research
            |-- Decisions & Outcomes
            |-- Private Customer Context
            |-- Additional Future Domains
The partitions aren't necessarily separate physical databases.

They're logical information environments with specific schemas, indexes, processing rules, and permissions.

The shared foundation allows information to be connected across partitions.

9. Contextual memory segmentation
Consider a person who is involved in technology, fashion, venture capital, and gaming.

The system should not retrieve their entire information history when answering a question about their documented venture investments.

It should retrieve the appropriate financial, professional, historical, and organizational context.

If evidence indicates that an idea originated in fashion and subsequently influenced a business decision, the system should expand into that domain.

This produces a dynamic context, constructed according to the question.

We need multiple retrieval mechanisms:

Exact entity and identifier matching.

Keyword and full-text search.

Semantic similarity.

Graph relationship traversal.

Temporal filtering.

Domain-specific retrieval.

Source reliability and evidence filtering.

No single retrieval method should control the whole architecture.

10. Evidence capsules
I propose testing a memory abstraction called an evidence capsule.

A capsule is a compact representation of an information context.

Example:

CAPSULE: AI_INFRASTRUCTURE_042

Relevant entities:
- Organization A
- Organization B
- Technology X

Domains:
- Technology
- Finance
- Infrastructure

Relevant events:
- Investment announcement
- Supplier expansion
- Product release

Assertions:
- Linked to original evidence

Hypotheses:
- Possible increase in downstream demand

Temporal range:
- Defined start and end dates

Access:
- Authorized scope

Cross-links:
- Other relevant capsules
Capsules are not permanent containers of truth.

They are rebuildable representations derived from original evidence.

When their evidence changes, affected capsules are updated.

This is important for keeping information compact while retaining historical accuracy.

11. Different kinds of memory need different retention policies
There should be no universal historical cutoff.

A technological investigation may benefit most from recent events.

A financial investigation may require decades of historical comparison.

A professional relationship may need a long history to understand how a collaboration developed.

We therefore need domain-specific policies for:

Retention periods.

Compression.

Historical summarization.

Source freshness.

Retrieval depth.

Relevance decay.

Legal deletion and correction.

Older information may be less important for one question but essential for another.

The system should preserve useful historical evidence where permitted, while allowing lower-priority derived representations to be compressed or regenerated.

PART IV — THE INFERENCE ENGINE
12. The core proprietary research
This is our highest technical priority.

The proposed research direction is:

Adaptive, evidence-grounded, cross-domain inference guided by documented reasoning frameworks.

The system should understand which information matters, retrieve it efficiently, connect it meaningfully, evaluate possible explanations, and learn from outcomes.

The methodology must be tested against existing approaches.

Simple questions shouldn't require thirteen expensive stages.

A factual lookup might only need retrieval and verification.

A complex attribution investigation may require the entire pipeline.

14. Investigation depth
The system should not investigate every possible connection indefinitely.

When it discovers a new relationship, it initially performs a limited investigation.

It gathers enough information to understand:

What was observed.

Which entities are involved.

Whether the relationship may be relevant.

What evidence exists.

What remains uncertain.

The relationship is then stored at an appropriate evidentiary status.

If it becomes relevant again, gains new evidence, or affects an important investigation, the system can investigate more deeply.

We should evaluate investigation priority using:

Expected information value, relevance, novelty, uncertainty, potential significance, and computational cost.

This is initially a heuristic, not a proven universal formula.

The objective is to maximize useful intelligence while controlling compute.

15. How hypotheses are handled
Suppose Executive A discusses Technology X.

Six months later, their company acquires a business working on Technology X.

Possible explanations include:

The conversation contributed to the acquisition.

The acquisition was already under consideration.

Both events were caused by wider industry developments.

Several independent factors contributed.

The model should maintain these explanations separately.

Each hypothesis needs:

Supporting evidence.

Contradictory evidence.

Relevant historical comparisons.

Missing information.

An uncertainty estimate.

A revision history.

Conditions that would strengthen or weaken it.

The system should not force one explanation simply because it sounds convincing.

16. Attribution
We will distinguish four attribution problems.

Type	Question
Content attribution	Where was information first observed?
Pathway attribution	Through which observable sources did it travel?
Causal attribution	What evidence suggests one event influenced another?
Outcome attribution	How did particular factors contribute to measurable results?
We will measure them independently.

Complete attribution is often impossible because relevant information may be private, unavailable, or never recorded.

The goal is to improve the quality and calibration of partial attribution.

The system must never mistake a plausible historical explanation for a proven cause.

17. The verification system
We need separate measurements of:

Source authenticity.

Evidence reliability.

Source independence.

Assertion support.

Relationship support.

Information prominence.

Information novelty.

Temporal relevance.

Predictive performance.

A rumor spreading widely is evidence of attention.

It is not necessarily evidence that the rumor is true.

Rumors may be retained as clearly labeled observations because their propagation can itself be informative.

Unverified assertions must not silently become established facts or contaminate downstream conclusions.

18. Autonomous learning
The system updates itself as new evidence arrives.

However, updating its knowledge and training its model weights are different processes.

Knowledge updates can occur continuously.

Model training and major inference-policy updates should be evaluated before deployment.

I recommend seven learning loops:

Learning loop	What improves
Extraction	Identifying claims, events, and relationships
Identity resolution	Matching entities accurately
Retrieval	Finding relevant information efficiently
Attribution	Distinguishing supported relationships from speculation
Framework selection	Choosing useful analytical methods
Forecasting	Improving probability calibration and predictions
Investigation planning	Allocating research effort efficiently
The system must record errors, corrections, and outcomes.

Improvement is an objective to measure, not a guarantee that every new prediction becomes more accurate.

PART V — HUMAN INTELLIGENCE AND EXPERT FRAMEWORKS
19. Understanding human decision-making
We will maintain two complementary representations:

Individual decision records

What was decided, what evidence was publicly or voluntarily disclosed, what alternatives were considered, and what happened afterward.

Evolving decision-pattern models

Tentative summaries of documented professional decision-making patterns across multiple cases.

The second never replaces the first.

A person can act differently in different circumstances.

The system should not assume a fixed personality explains all decisions.

20. What information about people matters?
Relevant information may include:

Professional roles and experience.

Public work and projects.

Explicitly expressed positions.

Published interests and expertise.

Verified professional affiliations.

Public collaborations and interactions.

Documented decisions.

Relevant organizational relationships.

Self-reported decision criteria.

Historical outcomes.

Public information sources they explicitly reference.

Private information can enrich an individual's own workspace when authorized.

We should distinguish verified relationships from speculative social associations.

We should also distinguish professional behavior from private psychological or political profiling. The system can study publicly documented information pathways and decisions without assigning sensitive hidden beliefs to individuals.

PART VI — INFORMATION ACQUISITION
23. The complete information-source universe
We should design the architecture to support many source types, even though the prototype will only implement a few.

Public and institutional information

Company filings, public contracts, government expenditure, legal-entity registries, published research, patents, publications, official announcements, public interviews, and historical archives.

Media and narrative information

Licensed news, public commentary, articles, videos, transcripts, podcasts, and permitted social-platform data.

Economic information

Investments, acquisitions, ownership, corporate relationships, financial disclosures, contracts, supply chains, procurement, and market indicators.

Professional information

Public professional profiles, organizational roles, research contributions, published collaborations, and documented business activity.

Authorized private information

Company documents, CRM data, meeting records, internal research, approved communications, and selected personal AI context.

Original research

Expert interviews, surveys, user research, direct verification, and independent investigations.

Specialized commercial datasets

Private-market intelligence, financial feeds, business-relationship datasets, and other licensed sources.

This is the potential information scope, not permission to collect everything.

Actual ingestion must respect access rights, contractual restrictions, confidentiality, and applicable law.

24. Three information environments
Public and licensed intelligence
Information used under its permitted terms.

Private intelligence
Individual or enterprise data maintained within its authorized environment.

Shared contribution intelligence
Specific information deliberately approved for contribution.

Private customer data does not automatically enter the shared model.

Derived outputs must preserve the restrictions of their underlying evidence.

Users should be able to correct, export, and request deletion of their contributed private information. Treatment of independently obtained public personal data must follow applicable rights and legal obligations rather than an unconditional rule that public data is never removable.

25. Research-driven acquisition
We won't collect everything simply because it exists.

Our investigation engine should also guide what information to acquire next.

For example:

A question requires understanding an organizational investment relationship.

The system identifies missing information concerning the transaction.

It checks permitted public records and relevant licensed sources.

If still unresolved, it records the evidence gap.

Where worthwhile, a researcher can conduct an authorized interview or request additional documentation.

The acquired information is then incorporated into the evidence system.

Information acquisition becomes problem-directed rather than indiscriminate.

PART VII — PRODUCTS AND EXPERIENCE

What's missing

INFORMATION WORLD MODEL
Technical Architecture, Research Proposal & Engineering Review
Version: 0.1 — Technical Review Draft
Date: November 11, 2026
Status: Pre-implementation; architecture and research hypotheses, not a validated system
Intended reader: Technical co-founder / systems architect

1. Executive Technical Summary
We propose building a continuously evolving Information World Model (IWM): a system that represents observable relationships among people, information, organizations, economic activity, decisions, and events, and uses those relationships to reconstruct developments, investigate possible causes, and forecast outcomes.

The fundamental research hypothesis is:

A temporally structured, evidence-grounded, cross-domain information model can produce more useful inferences than conventional retrieval, isolated knowledge graphs, or unstructured LLM reasoning, while controlling retrieval and inference costs.

The project is not primarily a chatbot, financial terminal, social listening tool, or generic GraphRAG implementation.

It is an attempt to develop a reusable information representation, memory, and inference architecture from which multiple intelligence applications can be built.

The system should:

Ingest structured and unstructured information from multiple authorized sources.

Extract entities, claims, events, interactions, decisions, and relationships.

Resolve identities and preserve source-level provenance.

Organize information into interconnected, context-specific memory structures.

Discover and investigate relationships across different domains.

Distinguish directly observed connections from inferred or causal hypotheses.

Apply documented expert analytical frameworks where appropriate.

Reconstruct historical information states without future-data leakage.

Generate measurable predictions and compare them against outcomes.

Update its knowledge and inference processes as new information arrives.

Operate through a common API, initially exposed through a messaging-style interface.

Support private enterprise knowledge without mixing confidential customer information into shared intelligence.

The first implementation must prove the architecture on a small, dense, cross-domain historical dataset, not attempt internet-scale knowledge acquisition.

2. Why This Architecture Is Different
Most existing systems specialize in particular information tasks.

System category	Primary capability	Limitation relevant to our research
Conventional RAG	Retrieve documents to answer questions	Weak explicit temporal and relational modeling
Knowledge graphs	Represent entities and relationships	Often need additional machinery for provenance, uncertainty, and changing evidence
GraphRAG	Use graph structure to improve retrieval	Does not inherently solve causal attribution or outcome-based learning
Temporal context graphs	Preserve changing relationships and history	Do not automatically supply our intended cross-domain economic and human decision inference
Market intelligence	Analyze companies, transactions, and markets	Coverage and reasoning vary by provider
Narrative intelligence	Analyze information diffusion, actors, and communities	Not necessarily integrated with financial events, professional decisions, and private business context
General-purpose LLMs	Flexible language reasoning	Knowledge may be stale, incomplete, or difficult to verify independently
Several existing systems already implement substantial parts of our proposed architecture.

This is not an assertion that none of these capabilities exist.

Our proposed technical differentiation is the integration of source-level evidence, temporal relationships, adaptive context construction, explicit hypothesis tracking, expert reasoning methods, and empirical outcome evaluation.

The key question is whether that integration creates a measurable improvement.

That must be demonstrated experimentally.

3. Foundational Architectural Principles
3.1 Relationship-native rather than domain-native
The system should not privilege financial, social, organizational, or informational data as its fundamental unit.

Instead, it must support distinct types of relationships among heterogeneous objects.

A company investing in a supplier, an executive citing a publication, a researcher introducing an idea, and a product gaining adoption are different events.

Their relationships may become meaningful when investigated together.

We therefore need a shared information representation capable of expressing different relationship mechanisms without flattening them into generic, ambiguous edges.

3.2 Observation is not explanation
The system needs three independent epistemic categories.

Observation: What was recorded by a source.

Assertion: What that source claims or what an extraction system interprets it as saying.

Inference: What the system hypothesizes based on the available evidence.

An observation can be authentic while the underlying claim is false.

An inference can be plausible without being causally established.

These distinctions must be enforced in storage, retrieval, and response generation.

3.3 Time is foundational
Every significant observation, event, assertion, and relationship should support temporal reasoning.

At minimum, distinguish:

Event or valid time.

Publication time.

Ingestion time.

System knowledge time.

Revision or invalidation time.

The system must support point-in-time queries.

For example:

"What would the system have concluded on January 1, 2024, using only information available then?"

This is necessary for meaningful historical evaluation.

3.4 Evidence must remain reconstructable
No derived relationship, summary, hypothesis, or answer should become an independent source of truth without a traceable evidence chain.

Derived information should retain source identifiers, processing versions, and dependency information.

Graph projections and compressed summaries should be rebuildable.

3.5 Information access must be context-dependent
The system will eventually possess multiple information environments.

They may have different data models, index structures, permissions, update frequencies, and retention requirements.

A query should activate the relevant environments rather than process the entire network.

3.6 Learning and knowledge updating are different
The system can continuously incorporate new evidence without continuously retraining a foundation model.

Knowledge updates, inference-policy changes, statistical model updates, and neural fine-tuning should be separately controlled and evaluated.

4. Proposed High-Level Architecture
We propose a modular architecture divided into five functional systems.

A. Observation System
Responsible for information ingestion, source management, extraction, and normalization.

B. World Memory System
Responsible for identity, evidence, events, relationships, time, segmentation, and retrieval.

C. Inference System
Responsible for investigation planning, relationship discovery, hypothesis evaluation, and attribution.

D. Learning and Evaluation System
Responsible for historical replay, feedback, model evaluation, forecasting, and improving system behavior.

E. Product and Integration System
Responsible for user interaction, agent execution, messaging, APIs, private workspaces, and monitoring.

End-to-end pipeline
Information sources

↓ Source connectors and authorization

Raw evidence store

↓ Parsing and extraction

Entity resolution + event normalization

↓ Evidence registration

Temporal assertion and relationship ledger

↓ Domain projections and memory segmentation

Context retrieval and query planning

↓ Expert-framework selection and investigation

Hypothesis generation + evidence evaluation

↓ Historical comparison and forecasting

Response / monitoring / decision support

↓ Outcomes and corrections

Learning and evaluation feedback

The process is not strictly linear.

New evidence should update affected portions of memory and trigger relevant investigations.

An answer may also cause the system to discover missing information and initiate additional retrieval.

6. Entity Resolution
This is a major technical risk.

A large information network becomes unreliable if the system merges distinct people or organizations.

I propose a staged entity-resolution pipeline.

Stage 1 — Exact identification
Match authoritative identifiers when available.

Examples include legal entity identifiers, company registration numbers, stable public account identifiers, and known document IDs.

Stage 2 — Candidate generation
For unidentified entities, retrieve possible matches using:

Names and aliases.

Organizations.

Document context.

Geographic context where relevant.

Professional roles.

Stable source identifiers.

Semantic representations.

Stage 3 — Candidate scoring
Evaluate evidence for and against each match.

Contextual similarity should not override contradictory identity evidence.

Stage 4 — Resolution
Possible outcomes:

Match existing entity.

Create new entity.

Retain unresolved candidate identities.

Refer ambiguous cases for review.

Stage 5 — Reversibility
Identity merges must preserve the original source identities and merge history.

A mistaken merge should be reversible.

This is especially important because false identity connections can create many downstream relationships.

Technical recommendation: Store source-specific identity mappings separately from canonical entity records. Treat identity reconciliation as a versioned process rather than permanently rewriting all historical references.

7. Evidence and Provenance Architecture
I recommend a provenance system based on explicit dependency records.

Every derived object should answer:

Which original observations support it?

Which transformation created it?

Which model and prompt version were used?

Which other derived objects did it depend on?

What evidence contradicts it?

Can it be reconstructed?

The result is a provenance dependency graph separate from the semantic world graph.

That distinction matters.

A semantic connection such as "Company A supplies Company B" is different from the computational connection "Assertion 103 was generated from Source 55 by Extractor v2."

Both graphs are necessary.

Proposed evidence states
Observed.

Extracted.

Proposed.

Supported.

Disputed.

Superseded.

Retracted.

Not every status transition should be automatic.

Evidence can strengthen an assertion without establishing causality.

Source independence
The system must distinguish:

Independent corroboration: Separate sources with independently obtained evidence.

Dependent repetition: Multiple sources repeating one original source.

A claim repeated 500 times may be highly prominent without being independently corroborated.

For narrative intelligence, all 500 repetitions may be relevant.

For factual verification, they may contribute little independent evidence.

Prominence, reliability, and corroboration must therefore be stored separately.

Provenance standards
We should investigate W3C PROV-O as a conceptual foundation for provenance relationships and OpenLineage for tracking data-processing jobs.

Neither should replace our domain-specific evidence semantics.

8. Temporal Memory and Historical Reconstruction
We need a bitemporal or stronger event-time/knowledge-time model.

For each important fact or relationship, distinguish:

Valid time: When the fact applied in the world.

Knowledge time: When the system had access to that information.

For example:

An investment occurred in January.

A public filing disclosed it in March.

Our system ingested the filing in April.

A historical query simulating February must not use that April-ingested record as knowledge available in February.

This remains true even if the event itself occurred in January.

Implementation implications
Preserve source publication timestamps.

Preserve ingestion timestamps.

Store revisions rather than overwriting history.

Support as_of queries.

Maintain provenance for historical states.

Prevent future information from leaking through summaries and embeddings.

Version inference outputs and predictions.

This becomes the foundation for historical backtesting.

Incremental updates
When new evidence arrives:

Identify affected entities, claims, and events.

Update canonical records.

Re-evaluate dependent relationships.

Invalidate affected derived summaries.

Refresh affected indexes and graph projections.

Reassess impacted hypotheses.

Trigger relevant monitoring or evaluation jobs.

We should avoid rebuilding the entire information model after every new observation.

9. Memory Segmentation
This is one of the primary proprietary research areas.

The central problem is:

How can we preserve broad interconnected information while retrieving only the relevant portion needed for a specific investigation?

We need to distinguish logical segmentation from physical database sharding.

Initially, I propose logical partitions based on:

Domain.

Entity.

Event.

Time window.

Information source.

Customer or permission scope.

Contextual relevance.

These dimensions can overlap.

An observation about an executive discussing an investment should be accessible through relevant financial, organizational, and information-network contexts.

It should not be duplicated as three independent facts.

Shared identity spine
Maintain one canonical identity system with source-specific mappings.

Domain projections can reference the same canonical entity without duplicating its identity.

Evidence capsules
I propose experimenting with a derived structure called an evidence capsule.

A capsule contains:

Relevant entity references.

Related observations.

Assertions and events.

Typed relationships.

Temporal range.

Summary representation.

Source dependencies.

Permission labels.

Cross-links to related capsules.

Model and construction version.

Capsules are materialized views, not the authoritative source of truth.

We should compare several capsule-construction strategies:

Entity-centered: Information associated with a particular entity.

Event-centered: Information surrounding a particular event.

Domain-centered: Information associated with a topic or industry.

Community-centered: Related information discovered through graph structure.

Dynamic: Context assembled for a specific query.

It is not yet established which is superior.

Research problem
The segmentation system should optimize:

Retrieval relevance.

Evidence coverage.

Cross-domain recall.

Latency.

Token usage.

Index-maintenance cost.

Historical correctness.

The risk is that aggressive segmentation will suppress weak but important cross-domain connections.

We need to benchmark that explicitly.

10. Query Planning and Context Routing
The inference engine should not immediately send the user's question to an LLM with a large collection of retrieved documents.

Instead, it should construct an investigation plan.

Example query
"Investigate how a major AI infrastructure development connects relevant executives, suppliers, capital commitments, and public technical discussions."

The query planner should:

Classify the question.

Resolve named entities.

Determine the relevant time window.

Identify required evidence types.

Select applicable domain indexes.

Retrieve initial evidence.

Identify unresolved connections.

Decide whether further investigation is justified.

Apply suitable analytical frameworks.

Produce a source-grounded conclusion.

Retrieval methods
The system should support a combination of:

Exact identifier retrieval.

Full-text search.

Vector similarity.

Typed graph traversal.

Temporal filtering.

Structured analytical queries.

Evidence-strength filtering.

Cross-domain expansion.

Adaptive investigation depth
We should not use a fixed traversal depth for all questions.

A simple factual query might need one or two retrieval operations.

A cross-domain attribution investigation may require several rounds.

I propose a bounded expansion policy using:

Expected information value, unresolved uncertainty, relevance, significance, and computational cost.

The system should stop expanding when additional retrieval is unlikely to materially change the answer or when the authorized investigation budget is exhausted.

This is a research hypothesis, not an established optimal algorithm.

11. Inference Engine Design
I recommend separating inference into specialized operations rather than building a single unrestricted autonomous agent.

11.1 Extraction
Identify entities, claims, events, references, and relationships from source material.

Use constrained structured outputs and schema validation.

11.2 Relationship discovery
Detect possible connections from:

Explicit references.

Shared entities.

Chronological sequences.

Common events.

Repeated information patterns.

Documented organizational or financial links.

Semantic similarity.

Semantic similarity alone must never establish a causal relationship.

11.3 Hypothesis generation
Construct alternative explanations for relevant observations.

11.4 Hypothesis evaluation
Retrieve supporting and contradicting evidence.

Evaluate assumptions and known confounding factors.

11.5 Attribution analysis
Separate:

Source attribution.

Information-pathway attribution.

Causal attribution.

Outcome attribution.

11.6 Consequence analysis
Investigate plausible second-order effects and dependencies.

For example:

A company announces a major infrastructure investment.

The system can inspect documented supplier relationships, technical capacity requirements, financing arrangements, and related industry developments.

It can identify possible downstream effects.

It must distinguish confirmed contracts from inferred opportunities.

11.7 Response generation
Generate a conclusion containing:

Answer.

Supporting evidence.

Relevant relationships.

Alternative explanations.

Uncertainty.

Missing information.

Possible next developments.

The final response should be independently traceable to the underlying investigation record.

13. Forecasting and Learning
This is a major requirement of the initial prototype.

Confidence or probability.

A prediction should be committed before its outcome becomes known.

Run the investigation.

Store the prediction.

Reveal subsequent outcomes.

Score the prediction.

Record failures and missing evidence.

Compare against baseline methods.

Accuracy.

Evidence recall.

Attribution quality.

Cost per investigation.

Latency.

User utility.

Attribution-model improvement.

28. Critical Technical Risks
These are the areas where we specifically want an experienced engineer to challenge our assumptions.

Risk 1 — Inference may not improve with graph complexity
Adding graph structure does not guarantee better reasoning.

A simpler retrieval pipeline may outperform the proposed architecture on many questions.

Required test: Controlled comparison against hybrid RAG and GraphRAG.

Risk 2 — Ground truth for attribution is scarce
Historical observation does not reveal every information exposure or private decision process.

Required mitigation: Separate factual and causal claims. Use direct evidence, explicit alternative hypotheses, and calibrated uncertainty.

Risk 3 — Entity-resolution mistakes compound
One incorrect merge may generate hundreds of false connections.

Required mitigation: Conservative resolution, reversible merges, identity confidence, and downstream dependency tracking.

Risk 4 — Context segmentation may lose important information
Selective retrieval could miss weak but valuable cross-domain links.

Required test: Compare adaptive partitions against unsegmented retrieval and broader graph search.

Risk 5 — Source quality may dominate model quality
A sophisticated inference engine cannot reliably reconstruct unavailable events.

Required mitigation: Track evidence coverage and acknowledge unresolved gaps.

Risk 6 — Model-generated relationships can become self-reinforcing
If generated hypotheses are treated as source evidence, the model may amplify its own errors.

Required mitigation: Strict separation between original observations, derived assertions, and hypotheses.

Risk 7 — Historical evaluations can leak future information
Later publications, current summaries, or revised indexes may contain knowledge unavailable at the target date.

Required mitigation: Knowledge-time filtering throughout retrieval, summarization, and forecast generation.

Risk 8 — Continuous enrichment may become expensive
Unbounded extraction, relationship discovery, and autonomous investigations could produce high recurring compute costs.

Required mitigation: Incremental processing, relevance gating, caching, bounded exploration, and cost observability.

Risk 9 — Private information can leak through derived outputs
A cross-domain model could accidentally reveal confidential customer information through summaries or relationships.

Required mitigation: Authorization-aware derivation, access-scope propagation, and tenant-isolation testing.

Risk 10 — The proprietary architecture may not be novel
Many proposed components already exist individually.

Required mitigation: Define specific technical claims and compare their measured performance against established systems.

Risk 11 — The first prototype may overreach
Attempting to implement the complete long-term vision could prevent us from validating any core hypothesis.

Required mitigation: A small but structurally representative test environment.

Risk 12 — A useful system may still lack a customer
Technical performance does not establish willingness to pay.

Required mitigation: Customer discovery and paid pilot discussions in parallel with engineering.

31. Final Technical Thesis
We are proposing an evidence-native, temporal, relationship-oriented intelligence architecture capable of representing and investigating developments across heterogeneous information environments.

The system combines:

Structured source observations.

Canonical entity resolution.

Temporal events and typed relationships.

Provenance and epistemic state.

Segmented, cross-domain memory.

Adaptive retrieval and investigation.

Explicit hypothesis and attribution tracking.

Expert analytical frameworks.

Historical reconstruction.

Forecasting and outcome-based evaluation.

Continuous, bounded learning.

Secure and portable interfaces.

The objective is not simply to generate better prose from retrieved documents.

It is to create a system that can reconstruct how information and real-world events relate, explain what the evidence supports, identify what remains uncertain, and improve its analytical usefulness through measured feedback.

The central technical question:

Can this architecture produce measurably better cross-domain explanations, historical reconstructions, and forecasts than simpler systems at an economically acceptable cost?

That is the question we need the first prototype—and this technical review—to answer.

PART 1 — WHERE THE IDEA ORIGINATED
1. My original concern: information, influence, and AI
The idea originally developed from my interest in the security of AI and the role of information in society.

I had been thinking about how people receive information, how their understanding develops, and how external information can shape their decisions without them necessarily recognizing the influence.

Historically, people obtained information through conversations, media, newspapers, search engines, institutions, communities, and other people.

Now, increasingly, people ask AI systems for explanations and recommendations.

An AI assistant doesn't simply provide a list of information.

It generates a coherent answer.

That answer involves selection, interpretation, prioritization, and framing.

This creates an important concern.

What happens when a growing portion of society relies on AI systems to understand events, evaluate arguments, form opinions, and make decisions?

What happens when people cannot easily determine why an AI selected particular information, what evidence supports it, or whether its framing is balanced?

Could an AI system influence public perceptions, purchasing decisions, or social narratives through its responses?

The possibility interested me from an AI security and information-integrity perspective.

I became interested in independent systems capable of observing how information is formed, distributed, interpreted, and potentially used to influence people.

This does not mean I am assuming specific companies or governments are secretly manipulating particular events.

The underlying problem is that information selection and influence are difficult to observe and independently verify.

The original motivation was to understand how information influences human understanding and behavior.

That remains fundamental to the project.

2. Narrative economics
The next major influence was narrative economics.

I became interested in how ideas move between people.

A person introduces an idea.

Other people encounter it.

Some repeat it. Some change its meaning. Some challenge it. Others carry it into different communities.

Eventually, the idea may become part of a larger conversation.

Sometimes it becomes a dominant narrative.

Sometimes it disappears.

Sometimes it returns years later in a different form.

This raised several questions:

Where did an idea originate?

Who first introduced it into a particular information environment?

Who amplified it?

Why did certain people repeat it?

What made the idea credible or interesting to a community?

How did it cross from one group to another?

Why did the narrative become prominent?

Why did it eventually decline?

What changed because of it?

I became especially interested in the role of individuals.

Some people have enormous audiences.

Others have much smaller audiences but consistently introduce ideas that are subsequently discussed by larger accounts.

That suggests influence isn't identical to popularity.

We need to understand the structure through which information moves.

An influential person might be valuable because they originate ideas, introduce information between communities, possess professional credibility, or participate in important organizational decisions.

The goal became understanding information propagation, not merely measuring engagement.

3. The problem of attribution
This led directly to attribution.

Suppose a small creator publishes an idea.

A much larger creator discusses something similar several days later.

That alone doesn't prove copying or influence.

Both may have encountered the same third source.

They may have independently reacted to the same event.

The connection may be coincidental.

But suppose similar sequences repeatedly appear across historical information.

That becomes something worth investigating.

Could we use timing, explicit references, semantic similarity, documented interactions, and historical patterns to reconstruct possible information pathways?

Could we gradually improve those reconstructions?

The important realization was:

Complete causal attribution is impossible when relevant information is missing, but partial attribution can still be useful and measurable.

We don't need to know everything.

We need to become increasingly good at distinguishing supported connections from unsupported explanations.

That became one of the foundations of the inference system.

PART 2 — WHY PEOPLE BECAME SO IMPORTANT
4. Information is carried through people
The next realization was that information networks are fundamentally connected to human activity.

People publish information, interpret it, discuss it, challenge it, make decisions, and transmit it between different environments.

Someone may learn something through a podcast, talk about it in a meeting, reference it in a blog, and eventually apply it to a business decision.

A conventional information system may record the podcast, publication, and business decision independently.

But the relationships between them can be difficult to reconstruct.

I became interested in modeling those connections.

Importantly, people don't belong to only one community.

A founder may be interested in technology, startups, design, gaming, fashion, and several other fields.

An idea encountered in fashion might influence a technology product.

A business opportunity might originate from a conversation outside the person's main industry.

A useful information network needs to understand those overlaps.

5. People should not be treated as single-category objects
An early architectural idea was to organize people into communities.

I realized that was insufficient.

A person can participate in many information environments simultaneously.

Their relevance changes according to the question being investigated.

For example, suppose a person participates in five professional or interest domains.

We should maintain their connections to those domains without retrieving their entire information history for every query.

If the investigation concerns venture investments, we retrieve relevant financial and professional information.

If the investigation concerns the origin of a design idea, we retrieve relevant publications, discussions, and design-related observations.

If the two domains intersect, we connect them.

People should be represented as intersections of relationships and contexts, not as members of mutually exclusive communities.

This became a major reason for introducing information segmentation.

6. Understanding professional decision-making
The project then moved beyond tracking who said what.

I started thinking about why people make decisions.

Why does one executive invest in a particular technology while another rejects it?

Why does a founder pursue one business opportunity instead of another?

Why do certain investors recognize opportunities before other investors?

How do people decide which information matters?

I don't want the system to simply assign permanent personality labels.

I want it to investigate documented decisions, explicitly expressed reasoning, historical actions, relevant circumstances, and outcomes.

For example, if an executive repeatedly describes using intuition in certain decisions, the model could investigate that professional decision-making pattern.

If the executive behaves differently in financial negotiations, the model should recognize that the decision context has changed.

We agreed that the system should maintain both:

A. Individual historical decision records.

B. Evolving, evidence-backed representations of observable decision-making patterns.

One shouldn't replace the other.

The goal is understanding decision processes and information relationships—not pretending to know someone's private mental state.

7. Why human psychology remains relevant
I don't want the psychological dimension removed from the project.

Human decisions are affected by attention, familiarity, trust, incentives, uncertainty, social context, emotions, and prior experiences.

Those mechanisms matter when investigating how narratives emerge and how decisions develop.

But we need to distinguish general behavioral research and observable professional patterns from unsupported claims about a particular individual's hidden beliefs or vulnerabilities.

A meaningful human-understanding system should ask:

What information was available to the person?

What professional interests and responsibilities are documented?

What did they explicitly say?

What decisions did they make?

What circumstances surrounded those decisions?

What alternative explanations might account for their behavior?

What happened afterward?

When authorized, direct interviews can add context that public records don't provide.

This is especially important because we will never possess every private conversation in the world.

PART 3 — THE PALANTIR INSPIRATION
8. Operational intelligence versus information intelligence
Another important influence was Palantir.

What interested me was the idea of connecting large amounts of fragmented information into a system that supports meaningful decisions.

Palantir's products operate in domains involving organizations, operations, processes, assets, and complicated information environments.

I began thinking about an adjacent problem.

What would a system look like if its primary objective were understanding the information environment itself?

Rather than merely answering:

"What assets does an organization possess?"

It might answer:

"How did this information emerge, who participated in its development, what decisions followed, and what relationships might explain those decisions?"

The ambition was not to copy Palantir.

It was to explore whether a similarly serious form of relational intelligence could be developed for information propagation, human activity, organizations, and economic relationships.

9. The intelligence world is larger than online information
At this point, I realized that public websites alone could never represent the complete information environment.

Important knowledge also exists inside organizations, private professional networks, meetings, research groups, and conversations.

For example, market information may circulate privately long before it becomes publicly documented.

A CEO might learn something through a legitimate industry discussion.

An investor might identify a business opportunity through a private professional network.

A researcher might possess original knowledge that hasn't been formally published.

This made authorized private information an important potential part of the architecture.

But it also introduced the need for strict information ownership, permissions, and separation between customer-private knowledge and shared intelligence.

PART 4 — HOW ECONOMICS ENTERED THE IDEA
10. Following money trails
A discussion with my former co-founder introduced an important perspective:

Follow the money.

Instead of investigating only information and narratives, follow capital flows, ownership structures, corporate relationships, investments, purchases, contracts, and economic dependencies.

For example, consider a hypothetical AI infrastructure ecosystem.

One company invests in AI infrastructure.

A different company supplies compute.

Another supplies equipment.

Another develops data centers.

Other companies provide electricity, cooling, construction, or financing.

Information and capital flow through these organizations.

We can investigate the documented relationships between them.

This may reveal business opportunities that aren't obvious when examining any single company in isolation.

However, announced investments, completed transactions, purchases, and equity ownership must be represented as different relationship types.

The system must not infer actual cash movements from announcements alone.

11. Second-order economic intelligence
The next important question was:

What happens beyond the immediate investment?

Suppose an industry receives substantial investment.

What infrastructure does it require?

Which suppliers could experience increased demand?

Which companies depend on those suppliers?

What constraints could delay the expansion?

Which business opportunities might emerge?

Could an information system discover economically meaningful second- or third-order relationships?

This introduced an important potential commercial application.

Investors and enterprises already pay for research on companies, markets, competitors, and supply chains.

If our system can connect information and relationships across domains more effectively, it might provide a useful advantage.

But I don't want the company reduced to a money-trail product.

Money trails are one of the clearest ways to connect information to measurable real-world consequences. They are not the entire objective.

12. The important correction: no hierarchy between people and economics
During earlier architecture discussions, the emphasis repeatedly shifted.

Sometimes toward people.

Sometimes toward narratives.

Sometimes toward money trails.

I rejected that.

These aren't independent priorities arranged in a hierarchy.

They're connected dimensions of the same world.

A person's information environment may influence a decision.

A financial incentive may influence what the person chooses to investigate.

A company decision may influence a market.

A market change may create a new narrative.

That narrative may influence another decision.

The relationships are multidirectional and evolving.

The architecture must understand the connections rather than privilege one category.

That is one of the strongest principles I've established.

We initially identified six dimensions:

They will expand.

For example, future domains may include scientific research, infrastructure, geography, regulation, products, industrial supply chains, institutions, and other relevant environments.

15. Relationships are fundamental, but they have distinct mechanisms
A financial transaction isn't the same thing as a conversation.

A public reference isn't the same thing as an influence pathway.

An acquisition isn't the same thing as an announcement.

A relationship between an organization and a large group of customers isn't the same as a relationship between two executives.

Our model must distinguish the mechanisms through which entities interact.

Typed relationships.

Context.

Assertions.

Evidence.

Hypotheses.

Decisions.

Outcomes.

Historical revisions.

The structure is relationship-native, but not every relationship should be represented as one simplistic graph edge.

Complex events need their own records, participants, roles, properties, and temporal states.

PART 6 — THE CORE TECHNICAL PHILOSOPHY
16. The proprietary asset should be the inference and memory architecture
The highest-priority technical research is not the messaging interface, dashboard, or collection of APIs.

It is how we represent information, construct memory, connect different information contexts, and reason over them.

I want to investigate whether we can create a distinctive system that:

Understands what information is relevant to a question.

Retrieves that information economically.

Connects it across different environments.

Identifies meaningful relationships.

Generates possible explanations.

Evaluates those explanations against evidence.

Predicts outcomes where possible.

Learns from what actually happens.

The proprietary advantage must be demonstrated, not assumed.

Existing systems already solve parts of this.

We should reuse good infrastructure rather than rebuild everything.

But I want the inference methodology and memory architecture to be areas where we conduct real research.

17. Segmented memory
I do not want a single system that retrieves millions of unrelated records whenever someone asks a question.

The system should maintain specialized information environments.

For example:

Professional and organizational networks.

Narrative and publication networks.

Financial and economic networks.

Technology and research networks.

Historical decision networks.

Private customer knowledge.

Other relevant domains.

These are not necessarily separate databases.

They're logical partitions connected through common identities, evidence, events, and relationships.

If a question requires financial and organizational information, the model should begin there.

If that investigation discovers a potentially important information pathway, it should expand into the narrative or publication network.

It should activate information according to the investigation, not an arbitrary fixed category.

18. A shared identity foundation
Every identifiable entity should have a stable internal identifier.

For example:

person_1042

organization_551

event_903

External names and aliases map to those identifiers.

However, creating identifiers does not solve identity ambiguity.

We need entity resolution, evidence-backed matching, and reversible merges.

Two people with identical names must not be automatically treated as one person.

The system should also recognize when multiple verified identifiers belong to the same person or organization.

19. Evidence-based memory
We shouldn't rely exclusively on model-generated summaries.

Summaries compress information and can lose important details.

The system needs durable references to underlying observations.

One architecture proposal is to maintain:

Canonical records: Original observations, entities, assertions, events, and permissions.

Derived structures: Graph projections, semantic indexes, analytical summaries, and contextual memory.

Hypothesis records: Proposed explanations, supporting evidence, contradictions, and uncertainties.

Derived structures should be reconstructable from canonical evidence.

20. Evidence capsules
We've discussed an experimental abstraction called an evidence capsule.

An evidence capsule is a compact, versioned representation of an information context.

It might contain:

Relevant entities.

Events.

Claims.

Relationships.

Source references.

Time boundaries.

Permissions.

Contextual summaries.

Cross-domain links.

Capsules can be assembled around entities, events, communities, subjects, or particular investigations.

The idea is to preserve the most useful structured context without processing the entire information network.

This is not yet proven to outperform existing retrieval methods.

We need to compare it against conventional RAG, hybrid retrieval, and existing GraphRAG.

PART 7 — HOW INFERENCE SHOULD WORK
21. What the system should actually do with information
The proposed reasoning cycle is:

Step 1 — Understand the question.

Identify the objective, relevant subjects, context, time window, and required answer type.

Step 2 — Choose relevant information environments.

Select the necessary domains and information indexes.

Step 3 — Retrieve evidence.

Use exact lookup, semantic search, graph traversal, temporal filtering, and structured queries.

Step 4 — Construct relevant relationships.

Connect information from different sources and contexts.

Step 5 — Identify possible explanations.

Generate candidate hypotheses for the observed relationships.

Step 6 — Challenge the explanations.

Look for contradictions, shared causes, missing information, and alternative explanations.

Step 7 — Decide whether more investigation is useful.

Expand only when further retrieval is likely to improve the answer.

Step 8 — Produce an explanation.

Distinguish documented facts, inferences, uncertainty, and possible implications.

Step 9 — Record the investigation.

Preserve the evidence, reasoning method, model version, and conclusion.

Step 10 — Learn from new evidence and outcomes.

Update affected hypotheses and evaluate prediction performance.

Not every question requires all ten steps.

The system should use the minimum necessary computation for the task.

22. The attribution problem
We identified four distinct forms of attribution.

Content attribution: Where was a particular idea or claim first observed?

Pathway attribution: Through which documented or plausible paths did information travel?

Causal attribution: Did one event meaningfully contribute to another?

Outcome attribution: How did certain developments contribute to a measurable result?

These should be evaluated separately.

A chronological sequence is not automatically a causal explanation.

Even repeated patterns can result from shared external factors.

The model must maintain competing explanations where necessary.

23. Missing information
Missing evidence is unavoidable.

Suppose we know an executive discussed a technology publicly and their company subsequently acquired a business in that field.

We don't know whether the discussion influenced the acquisition.

The system should investigate several explanations.

It should also identify what additional evidence could distinguish them.

If direct verification is possible and worthwhile, an authorized researcher might interview the relevant people or review additional documents.

The system cannot independently reconstruct every private conversation.

The objective is improved reasoning under incomplete observability.

24. Autonomous investigation
I don't want researchers involved in every small update.

When the system discovers a possible connection, it should initially perform a limited investigation.

It collects enough information to understand whether the connection is worth maintaining.

If the same subject repeatedly appears or the relationship becomes important, the system can investigate more deeply.

Research effort should be related to:

Relevance.

Novelty.

Evidence strength.

Uncertainty.

Significance.

Expected information gain.

Computational cost.

This prevents every small observation from triggering an expensive investigation.

Important unresolved cases can be referred to researchers.

PART 8 — THE HUMAN EXPERTISE LAYER
25. A machine can process information, but what makes information important?
This is one of the most recent and important additions.

I want to study how extremely capable professionals approach information.

People such as:

Venture capitalists.

Management consultants.

Quantitative researchers.

Economists.

Corporate executives.

Security researchers.

Industry specialists.

Narrative and information researchers.

Two experts may study the same information and reach different conclusions because they ask different questions, weigh different evidence, and operate with different professional frameworks.

I want our model to understand these frameworks.

26. Expert reasoning frameworks
We should document:

What problem the expert is trying to solve.

Which information they seek.

Which signals matter.

How they verify them.

Which assumptions they use.

How they compare alternatives.

What makes them reject a hypothesis.

How they reach a decision.

How they evaluate the result.

These methods should become reusable analytical components.

For example, an investigation into a corporate acquisition might benefit from several frameworks:

Corporate strategy: What strategic objective could explain the acquisition?

Economics: What incentives and constraints matter?

Financial analysis: What monetary relationships and dependencies exist?

Information analysis: What public discussions and disclosures preceded it?

Historical analysis: What happened in similar situations?

The model should eventually select and combine frameworks automatically according to the investigation.

But expert advice must remain testable.

We cannot assume an analytical method is correct simply because it came from an experienced person.

27. How we obtain expert knowledge
We intend to combine:

Published professional methods.

Research papers.

Case studies.

Direct conversations with specialists.

Interviews with experienced operators.

Expert analysis of historical examples.

Actual observed outcomes.

Each framework should retain provenance, assumptions, limitations, version history, and evaluation results.

PART 9 — LEARNING AND HISTORICAL PREDICTION
28. The prototype must learn from mistakes
One of my strongest requirements is that the prototype demonstrate learning.

I want to observe the system processing information, making an inference, testing it, and identifying where it was wrong.

The system should improve through evidence and evaluation rather than simply generate plausible explanations.

29. Historical replay
Imagine choosing a historical cutoff date.

We provide the model only information that would have been available at that time.

We ask it what might happen next.

The model records its prediction.

Then we reveal what actually happened.

We compare the prediction against the outcome.

This must be repeated across multiple cases.

Critically, the system must not accidentally use later information when answering a historical question.

That requires distinct event-time and knowledge-time representations.

30. Different learning loops
The system should learn from different kinds of mistakes separately.

For example:

Identity mistake: Two different people were merged.

Improve entity resolution.

Extraction mistake: A financial commitment was interpreted as a completed payment.

Improve structured extraction.

Retrieval mistake: Important supporting evidence wasn't retrieved.

Improve context selection.

Attribution mistake: An unsupported information pathway was treated as causally established.

Improve hypothesis evaluation.

Prediction mistake: A forecast was poorly calibrated.

Improve the forecasting component.

Framework-selection mistake: The wrong analytical method was used.

Improve framework routing.

We should not treat every error as something requiring fine-tuning the same LLM.

31. Model training
I want to use existing open-source models and frameworks wherever appropriate.

We shouldn't waste time rebuilding solved infrastructure.

But I do want to investigate fine-tuning specialized components as we accumulate validated examples.

Possible specialized models include:

Entity extraction.

Entity resolution.

Relationship classification.

Semantic representations.

Evidence evaluation.

Hypothesis ranking.

Forecasting.

Context routing.

Training our own foundation model is not necessary for the first prototype.

The proprietary value may come from the entire inference and information architecture rather than one model checkpoint.

PART 10 — INFORMATION ACQUISITION
32. Potential information sources
We want the architecture to support a very broad range of possible information.

The theoretical source universe includes:

Research publications, articles, company announcements, official filings, public interviews, podcasts, technical documents, patents, historical archives, and authorized social-platform data.

Economic information

Investments, acquisitions, contracts, ownership records, financial disclosures, procurement records, supply chains, market indicators, and government expenditures.

Professional information

Private organizational information

Commercial data

Licensed financial and market databases, private-market research, and other specialized sources.

33. Data acquisition should become intelligent
Instead of collecting every possible source, the system should learn which sources and information types are useful for particular investigations.

For example:

A question concerns a relationship between two companies.

The system identifies the companies.

It looks for documented transactions, organizational relationships, professional roles, and relevant public statements.

If the evidence suggests that certain people or events matter, it expands the investigation.

The system should decide what information to acquire next based on its relevance to the research question.

That creates a feedback loop between acquisition and inference.

34. Unverified information
We agreed that unverified information is not automatically worthless.

A rumor may be false but still important to understanding information propagation.

Repeated claims may demonstrate public attention without independently proving truth.

The system should therefore track:

Whether a claim was made.

Who or what source made it.

Whether the underlying assertion is supported.

Whether sources are independent.

How widely the claim spread.

Which relationships are documented.

Which explanations remain speculative.

Unverified claims may inform an investigation, but they must not silently become verified facts.

PART 11 — PRIVATE INFORMATION AND THE NETWORK
35. Three information environments
We currently distinguish:

1. Public and licensed intelligence

Information used according to applicable access and licensing rules.

2. Private customer intelligence

Authorized user or enterprise information kept within its own permission boundaries.

3. Explicit contribution intelligence

Information deliberately approved for broader sharing or collaboration.

These environments can use common technical infrastructure without automatically sharing their underlying data.

36. Why private information matters
Public information provides only part of the picture.

Authorized private documents and meeting records may help organizations understand their own decisions, relationships, projects, and historical knowledge.

But we cannot assume private knowledge should enter a shared global model.

This is both a security constraint and a business-design problem.

We need to investigate how products such as enterprise intelligence platforms and private-market research services obtain information, manage permissions, and make private data useful.

Possible approaches include customer-private inference, deliberate source-specific contributions, and properly licensed aggregated research.

The system should preserve the ability to generate customer-specific intelligence without disclosing protected information to other customers.

37. Selective enterprise wearables
I proposed an executive recording wearable.

An important correction: this is not a consumer hardware giveaway.

The concept is to offer a device selectively to high-value enterprise customers, executives, and trusted professional contacts.

For example, if an enterprise customer is paying $60,000–$100,000 annually, a hypothetical $60 device is not the main economic challenge.

The device could provide:

Authorized meeting recording.

Transcription.

Meeting summaries.

Decision extraction.

Follow-up tracking.

Searchable private memory.

Context for an enterprise intelligence agent.

The exact data retention and processing model is undecided.

Recording rights, participant authorization, enterprise confidentiality, and privacy requirements must be built into the design.

Hardware is a future extension, not a dependency for the first prototype.

2. REQUIREMENT PRIORITIES
Every major requirement should have one of four states.

State	Meaning
M0 — Core prototype	Must be exercised in the first functional demonstration

R — Research	Requires comparative experimentation before final selection

Component 6 — Expert Framework Engine
Represents and applies professional analytical methodologies.

Component 7 — Attribution and Prediction Engine
Investigates information pathways, explanatory mechanisms, potential consequences, and forecasts.

Component 8 — Learning and Evaluation Engine
Compares predictions against outcomes and improves evaluated system components.

Component 9 — Autonomous Monitoring
Watches relevant developments and triggers bounded investigations.

Component 10 — Interfaces and Applications
Exposes intelligence through messaging, APIs, CLI, MCP, and enterprise integrations.

Component 11 — Security and Governance
Controls access, licensing, private information boundaries, auditability, and permitted actions.

These components are logical modules.

A question crosses multiple domains	Retrieve relevant information across contexts

An entity appears in several domains	Preserve one appropriately resolved identity

40. FINAL SYSTEM REQUIREMENTS
The Information World Model must ultimately be:

Relationship-native

It represents heterogeneous interactions and their changing mechanisms.

Evidence-native

Every material conclusion has a traceable evidence basis or explicit uncertainty.

Temporal

It preserves historical states, revisions, and event sequences.

Cross-domain

It connects relevant information without privileging one category.

Context-sensitive

It retrieves and reasons over the information necessary for a particular question.

Autonomously updating

It incorporates new evidence and revises affected interpretations.

Evaluable

Its extraction, retrieval, attribution, and predictions can be measured.

Cost-aware

Research and inference operate under practical resource budgets.

Permission-aware

Private data and derived outputs retain access boundaries.

Model-independent

It can replace underlying models without losing its information.

Portable

Its canonical records, research, and specifications survive changes in tools.

Expandable

New information domains and capabilities can be added without rebuilding the foundation.
